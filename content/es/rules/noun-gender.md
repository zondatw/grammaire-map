## Explanation

Every Spanish noun has a grammatical gender — masculine or feminine. Gender affects the form of articles, adjectives, and pronouns.

**General rules:**
- Nouns ending in **-o** are usually **masculine**: *el libro*, *el gato*, *el vino*
- Nouns ending in **-a** are usually **feminine**: *la casa*, *la mesa*, *la planta*

**Always feminine** regardless of ending:
- Nouns ending in **-ción / -sión**: *la canción*, *la nación*, *la televisión*
- Nouns ending in **-dad / -tad / -tud**: *la ciudad*, *la libertad*, *la virtud*
- Nouns ending in **-umbre**: *la costumbre*, *la cumbre*

**Notable exceptions:**
- **el día** (day) — ends in -a but is masculine
- **la mano** (hand) — ends in -o but is feminine
- **el mapa**, **el problema**, **el tema**, **el sistema** — Greek-origin words ending in -a are masculine
- **la radio**, **la foto**, **la moto** — shortened forms are feminine

## Examples

| Spanish | English |
|---------|---------|
| el libro | the book (masc, -o) |
| la canción | the song (fem, -ción) |
| la ciudad | the city (fem, -dad) |
| el día | the day (exception: masc) |
| la mano | the hand (exception: fem) |
| el problema | the problem (Greek origin: masc) |
| la libertad | freedom (fem, -tad) |
| la costumbre | custom/habit (fem, -umbre) |

## Pitfall

Don't assume every noun ending in -a is feminine: *el día*, *el mapa*, *el problema*, *el sistema*, *el tema* are all masculine. Always learn a noun's gender together with its article when you first encounter it.
