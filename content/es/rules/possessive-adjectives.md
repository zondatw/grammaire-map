## Explanation

Possessive adjectives in Spanish agree with the **thing possessed** (not the possessor) in gender and number.

**Short (unstressed) forms — used before the noun:**

| Possessor | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| yo | mi | mis |
| tú | tu | tus |
| él/ella/usted | su | sus |
| nosotros | nuestro/nuestra | nuestros/nuestras |
| vosotros | vuestro/vuestra | vuestros/vuestras |
| ellos/ustedes | su | sus |

*Mi*, *tu*, and *su* do not change for gender — only for number.
*Nuestro/a* and *vuestro/a* change for both gender and number.

**Long (stressed) forms — used after the noun or with *ser*:**
*mío/mía/míos/mías*, *tuyo/tuya*, *suyo/suya*, *nuestro/nuestra*, *vuestro/vuestra*, *suyo/suya*

Used for emphasis or after the verb: *Un amigo mío* (a friend of mine). *La casa es nuestra.* (The house is ours.)

**Ambiguity of *su*:** *Su* can mean his, her, its, your (usted), their, your (ustedes). Context usually clarifies; otherwise use: *el libro de él*, *el libro de ella*, etc.

## Examples

| Spanish | English |
|---------|---------|
| mi libro | my book |
| mis libros | my books |
| nuestra casa | our house |
| nuestros amigos | our friends (masc/mixed) |
| su coche | his/her/your/their car |
| Es un amigo mío. | He is a friend of mine. |
| La decisión es vuestra. | The decision is yours. |

## Pitfall

English speakers often try to make possessive adjectives agree with the possessor's gender, but they agree with the noun possessed: *mi madre* (my mother) uses *mi*, not a feminine form — there is no feminine *mi*. Only *nuestro/a* and *vuestro/a* have separate masculine and feminine forms.
