## Explanation

The basic negation in Italian is **non**, placed directly before the verb.

*Parlo italiano.* → *Non parlo italiano.* (I don't speak Italian.)

**Double negatives** are grammatically required in Italian — unlike in English. When you use a negative word (*niente*, *nessuno*, *mai*, *più*, *ancora*), you must also keep **non** before the verb:

| Negative word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| niente / nulla | nothing | *Non ho niente.* (I have nothing.) |
| nessuno | nobody / no one | *Non viene nessuno.* (Nobody is coming.) |
| mai | never | *Non mangio mai la carne.* (I never eat meat.) |
| più | no longer / not anymore | *Non abito più qui.* (I no longer live here.) |
| ancora | not yet | *Non è ancora arrivato.* (He hasn't arrived yet.) |
| né ... né | neither ... nor | *Non mangio né carne né pesce.* |

The negative word can come before or after the verb. When it comes before, **non** is dropped:
*Nessuno viene.* / *Non viene nessuno.* (Nobody is coming.)

## Examples

| Italian | English |
|---------|---------|
| Non parlo francese. | I don't speak French. |
| Non ho niente da fare. | I have nothing to do. |
| Non andiamo mai in vacanza. | We never go on vacation. |
| Non ho ancora mangiato. | I haven't eaten yet. |
| Non lavora più qui. | She no longer works here. |

## Pitfall

English speakers often omit **non** when using a negative word like *niente* or *mai*, thinking the sentence is already negative. In Italian, *Ho niente* is incorrect — the double negative *Non ho niente* is the standard form.
